📊 City Economic Health Report: Boston

Boston earns a B- grade in my latest metro economic analysis, landing at the 49th percentile among America's 50 largest metropolitan areas. The grade reflects a city caught between its considerable strengths and the mounting costs of its own success.

The labor market tells a reassuring story. At 3.9%, unemployment runs half a percentage point below the major metro average, while labor force participation of 68.5% exceeds the benchmark by nearly four points. Boston workers remain engaged and employed, no small feat in this cacophonous economy.

Housing dynamics reveal a market under pressure from both ends. Homes sell in just 37 days, well ahead of the 54-day metro average, suggesting surplus demand. Building permits fell 26% year-over-year against a metro avg gain of 3%, a supply response that seems at odds with the evident appetite for Boston housing. Price per square foot rose 1.0% while the broader market declined, a divergence that underscores the metro's relative desirability even as affordability erodes.

The cost of living score of 6.46 against a 4.8 metro average quantifies what residents already know. Boston is very expensive. More notable, perhaps, is that wage growth of 2.1% trails the metro avg rate of 4.2% by half. In most markets, slower wage growth accompanies weaker demand for labor. In Boston, it accompanies tight employment, an unusual combination that may reflect sector-specific pressures in education and healthcare, or simply the limits of what employers believe they can pass through.

The picture that emerges is not of decline but of constraint. Boston's economic engine continues to generate employment and attract capital. Whether it can sustain that position while housing supply contracts and real wages stagnate is the question the next twelve months will answer.
