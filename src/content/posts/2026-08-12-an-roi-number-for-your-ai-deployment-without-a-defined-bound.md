An ROI number for your AI deployment without a defined boundary is either incomplete or totally wrong. How to fix it...

Before your AI ROI means anything, four things have to be clear: which costs count, which value counts, over what period, and whether the evaluation is based on observed or expected outcomes. Change any one of those borders of your boundary, and the ROI changes with it.

The deployment may be identical and the spending unchanged, but a different boundary can produce a different result. That is not a calculation error. It is what happens when the ROI question has not been fully defined.

This is also why two people can examine the same AI rollout and reach different conclusions about whether it paid for itself, without even disagreeing about the underlying facts. They may be using the same costs and the same outcomes. What differs is the boundary around the evaluation.

None of this determines whether an ROI should be positive or negative, or where an organization should draw its boundary. Those are management decisions. Nor does an evaluation require realized value when the organization is forecasting. Expected value can be used, provided it is identified as expected rather than observed.

Defining the boundary is part of the evaluation itself because the boundary determines what the ROI number actually means. It establishes which costs count, which benefits count, over what period, and whether the analysis is based on observed or expected outcomes.

Without those choices, the arithmetic may be correct, but the result is still ambiguous. Before asking whether an AI deployment produced a 20%, 50%, or 200% return, first define what that return is measuring.
