Why the “Better” Model Can Lose, aka why you should stop worrying and start to love open weight models...

Consider a company choosing between two AI models to route customer messages. The larger model is 96% accurate. The smaller model is 93% accurate. The larger model is therefore technically better. But that does not settle the core business problem, nor does it automatically win.

-- Why the Smaller Model Wins --

- The company is selecting a model for a specific task, not purchasing intelligence in the abstract. Spec to the work, never spec to the model.

- The task requires a 92% routing accuracy. The last 8% are uncertain and high-risk messages, which are sent to human reviewers.

- Both models exceed the required performance threshold. Both models would lead to a 92% reduction in the routing task.

- The larger model’s additional accuracy has economic value only if it materially improves the workflow, for example, by reducing labor, losses, delays, or risk.

- In this case, the additional three percentage points does none of these things. The same review team is still required for high-risk 8%, and customer outcomes remain effectively unchanged.

- The larger model costs four times as much to operate. Just look at costing for DeepSeek V4 Flash vs Opus 5. It is peanuts to elephants.

- Therefore, the larger model is technically superior but economically inferior. Its additional capability creates additional cost without creating corresponding excess value.

This conclusion would change if the higher accuracy reduced review labor, prevented costly mistakes, or materially improved customer outcomes. In that case, the added capability MIGHT justify the added cost.

The broader principle is simple:

Technical superiority matters only when it produces useful differences inside the actual workflow. Once multiple models satisfy the task’s requirements, the best model is the one that delivers the required outcome at the lowest total cost, not the one with the highest benchmark score.
