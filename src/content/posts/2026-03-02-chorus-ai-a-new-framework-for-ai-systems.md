I've written before about the rise of [Agentic AI](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/liberating-agentic-ai-through-limitation-daniel-wipert-t6ice/?trackingId=fiMRoPQbSrCbzqRpUIuBsQ%3D%3D). When I was studying alongside other business leaders at [Kellogg School of Management](https://www.linkedin.com/school/kellogg-school-of-management/posts/?feedView=all), one thing stood out above everything else: we are barely scratching the surface of what AI can do, and the next great leap forward will be Agentic AI. Then I stepped away. Work got busy. I spent more time teaching. Creative pursuits pulled me away.

Now that I'm stepping back in, one thing is crystal clear: I was right about Agentic AI. I was also very wrong about what Agentic AI actually means. I made the same mistake everyone else in AI has been making. We assumed the models would keep getting better, smarter, faster, and that eventually they'd handle complex tasks on their own. Self-contained Agents. Hence the name, Agentic AI. The problem with that thinking? It ignores the very foundations of Generative AI and its inherent limitations.

OpenAI and Anthropic are promising us superintelligence and AGI in months, not years. Yet they are still unable to answer even the most basic questions about the technology they are shipping every day. Bias. Hallucination. Explainability. These are not edge cases or future problems. These are the wobbling legs that threaten to collapse the first era of AI. They are structural failures built into the foundation of generative AI, and Silicon Valley has collectively decided to ignore them. Chasing the delusional utopianism of AGI is a lot more exciting than resolving these structural problems. Ignorance is also a lot more lucrative.

I have been working on a new framework and paradigm for business-ready Agentic AI that incorporates two key foundations:

One, single model systems are weak. Single models have inherent bias. They will absolutely hallucinate. Furthermore, they are totally incapable of validating their own outputs, which makes them unusable for most business tasks. Single-model LLMs are a massive liability for critical systems. This is why Gen AI is hitting a plateau in usage. ChatGPT will confidently make up legal cases to back up your claims and then double down when you question it. See below in the further reading section for the many examples of how Gen AI has already created chaos and havoc in real-world business and legal environments.

No AI business leader will tell you the truth about this, because the issue is unfixable. Gen AIs are LLMs, and LLMs will always hallucinate, have bias, and defy explainability. No matter how many billions of nodes they add to ChatGPT or Opus or Qwen or MiniMax, they will always be prone to bias and hallucination because they generate fluent, probability-based text rather than verified truth. They learn from human data that already contains these imperfections. Scale and polish tend to make these errors more convincing, not less. The practical solution is containment: grounding, verification, and structural constraints around the models.

AI needs to embrace and incorporate closed-loop system architecture. Closed-loop systems are not a new idea. They are the backbone of modern engineering. A thermostat reads the room temperature and adjusts. An autopilot checks its altitude and corrects. A manufacturing line inspects each unit and flags defects before they leave the floor. In every case, the output is fed back into the system, measured against a standard, and corrected if it falls short. This is called a feedback loop, and for over a century it has been the fundamental design principle behind everything from electrical grids to space navigation to cardiac monitors. We do not let critical systems run unchecked. We build in verification, correction, and accountability by design.

The last few years of AI development have been deluded by a myopic obsession with models. The entire industry (researchers, investors, journalists, and executives alike) became transfixed by the LLM arms race. How big could the model get? How many parameters could it hold? How fast could it reason? Benchmark after benchmark measured one model against another, as if raw capability was the only metric that mattered. Meanwhile, the foundational question of how the model’s output could be incorporated into real-world work systems was mostly ignored. This is like obsessing over the specific components of a car engine without even knowing where you want to go.

Chorus AI is my attempt to start building a new framework for AI: a multi-model, closed-loop architecture designed from the ground up around the two principles the industry has spent years ignoring. Not a bigger model. Not a faster model. But rather, a system of models that checks itself, challenges itself, and refuses to let an unverified output reach the end user. Models generate. Chorus AI governs. Open-loop AI assumes it’s right; Chorus AI proves that it is. This isn’t about beating GPT-5 or Opus at their own game. It’s about building the kind of intelligence they were never meant to deliver: dependable, auditable, and fit for real-world decisions and actual work.

[Dossier](https://github.com/danielwipert/chorus-ai-dossier) is the first application built on the Chorus AI framework, and it is designed to do one thing: take a document and produce an analytical report that is grounded, traceable, and defensible, the kind of output you could actually stake a decision on. Not a summary. Not a chatbot response. A structured intelligence product, built the way a good analyst would build one, with every claim tied back to a source and every gap explicitly acknowledged.

The [Dossier](https://github.com/danielwipert/chorus-ai-dossier) pipeline runs in seven locked stages, and no stage can proceed until the one before it has passed. The document is first ingested and segmented, then a dedicated extraction model pulls atomic facts directly from the text: no interpretation, no synthesis, just what the document actually says. Those facts are locked. Nothing downstream can change them. They become the grounded truth against which everything else is measured.

Then the closed-loop begins. Three independent summarization models (drawn from different vendors and architectures) each read the document without seeing each other's work and produce separate summaries. A dedicated verification model then scores each summary against the locked fact list, checking for coverage, unsupported claims, and alignment. Any summary that falls below the threshold gets regenerated and re-scored. The pipeline does not move forward until at least two summaries pass with a 100% rating on factual veracity. This is not error-checking as an afterthought. It is error-checking as architecture

The verified summaries then pass to two contextualizer models, which independently layer in external context (historical, methodological, comparative) before a compiler synthesizes the whole into a final [Dossier](https://github.com/danielwipert/chorus-ai-dossier) with six required sections, including a full audit trail that records every model that participated, every fact that was used, and every confidence score along the way. The result is an AI output that can actually tell you how it got there.

[Dossier](https://github.com/danielwipert/chorus-ai-dossier) is not just a proof of concept. It is a working system built on a simple but radical premise: that the way the industry has been building AI is wrong, and that closed-loop, multi-model architecture is a correction and best path forward. It does not compete with GPT or Opus on raw capability. It competes on something more important: reliability, traceability, and trust. Every stage gate, every verification loop, and every locked fact list exists for one reason: to produce an output you can actually trust.

On a morning in June 1985, a cancer patient in Marietta, Georgia sat down in front of a radiation therapy machine called the Therac-25 and received a dose of radiation 100 times what had been prescribed by the doctor. He died weeks later. Over the next two years, the machine installed in hospitals across the United States and Canada would injure and kill at least five more people in nearly identical incidents. Investigators eventually traced the cause to a software race condition, a timing error so subtle it only appeared when a technician typed too quickly.

The previous version of the Therac had hardware failsafes: physical interlocks that would catch exactly this kind of error. Engineers had removed them when they built the Therac-25, confident that the software alone was sufficient. There was no independent verification. No secondary check. No closed-loop. The machine did what it was told, and it was wrong. Nobody knew until the patients started dying.

What makes the Therac-25 disaster so instructive and uncomfortable is that it was not a story about a bad machine or a negligent engineer. It was a story about misplaced trust in an automated system that had no mechanism for self-correction. Forty years later, we are making the same catastrophic mistake at a civilization level. We are deploying AI into radiology, finance, legal analysis, medical diagnosis, and now military decisions.

The models are fluent and confident and, on their best days, genuinely impressive. But in most cases, nothing is checking the work. No verification loop. No circuit breaker. No closed-loop. The Therac-25 did not kill people, because it was malicious. It killed people because the system was never designed to ask what would happen when it was wrong. That question is not optional; it needs to be at the core of all Applied AI system design.

Every time you drive through a patch of black ice, your car catches itself before you can. A closed-loop system is saving your life. The Electronic Stability Control system, standard in every car sold in the United States since 2012, monitors wheel speed, steering angle, and lateral acceleration dozens of times per second. When it detects a skid forming, it does not wait for the driver to respond. It intervenes, selectively applying brakes and reducing engine power until the car is back under control. Most drivers never know it happened. The system is invisible precisely because it works. It was designed not just to act, but to verify, correct, and act again. It is not smarter than the driver, but it is accountable in a way the driver could never be.

This is the trust architecture AI has been missing, and it is the architecture Chorus AI is built to provide. The goal is not to replace frontier models or to pretend the remarkable capabilities of modern AI do not exist. The goal is to surround those capabilities with the same discipline that engineers have applied to every other critical system for the last century: verification, correction, and accountability by design.

[Dossier](https://github.com/danielwipert/chorus-ai-dossier) is the first proof that this is possible: a working system that takes a document, runs it through seven locked stages, seven independent models, and a closed verification loop, and produces an output that can tell you exactly how it got there. No hallucinations dressed up as confidence. No open loop mistaken for intelligence. Just a system that was designed, from the first line of code, to ask what happens when it is wrong, and to have an answer when it inevitably fails, like all systems do. The future of AI is not the black box model; it is the human-designed system.
