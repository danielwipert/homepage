Ok, now V3 of City Economic Report is live. The funny thing about releasing something is that you see all the flaws immediately...

Even though this project is not a high priority right now, I find myself very driven to perfect it. I started this maybe 8 months ago. It went from playing around with Python and public APIs to this elaborate and, I think, novel report on city economic health.

It has always blown my mind that economic news in America is all about national data, never about city data. We do not look at a national weather report, we look at a local weather report, because it applies to us and affects us. I think economic data should be similar. What is happening in New York City is not what is happening in Chicago or Peoria or Tacoma or Reno. Economics is local more than it is national or global. They are connected yes, but what applies to you and the people you know, that is local.

I decided to revamp the scoring here again. I am a stickler for accuracy and truthfulness. Nothing is going to be perfect, but I am looking for more truthful rather than less. I think I got there with this new scoring.

Here is what I am looking at for these scores:

This scoring model evaluates the economic health of U.S. metropolitan areas with a clear emphasis on labor market strength. Its foundation is a weighted composite of eight metrics, led by Labor Demand (25%), which blends employment growth with weekly hours to distinguish true expansion from workforce strain. This is supported by Unemployment (20%), a widely followed but lagging indicator, and Wage Growth (15%), which captures real-time labor market tightness. Together, these three metrics anchor the system around job creation, labor utilization, and compensation dynamics.

The model then incorporates structural and affordability factors. Cost of Living (12%) measures housing affordability relative to local wages using a composite of absolute levels, trends, and peer comparisons. Labor Force Growth (10%) tracks whether workers are entering or leaving the market, correcting for distortions in unemployment data. Building Permits (10%) provide a forward-looking signal of economic confidence and future housing supply, using smoothed data to reduce volatility.

Finally, two lower-weighted metrics provide additional context. Days on Market (5%) evaluates housing accessibility through a context-sensitive framework, while Office Economy (3%) measures the scale and growth of knowledge-based employment. These act as refinements rather than core drivers, ensuring the model captures economic nuance without over-penalizing structurally different metros. Overall, the system remains deliberately labor-centric, with supporting layers that assess affordability, supply, and economic composition.
