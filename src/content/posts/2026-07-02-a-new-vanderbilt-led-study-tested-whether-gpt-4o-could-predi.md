A new Vanderbilt-led study tested whether GPT-4o could predict which cancer patients were at risk of stopping medication early. After fine-tuning, GPT-4o outperformed the strongest traditional machine-learning model in the study.

That result is notable, but it is not the most important part. The more interesting finding is that the model could also produce explanations that a human team could inspect. It was not merely issuing a score. It was offering a reasoned account of what seemed to matter.

- Why This Matters Beyond Health Care

This is the threshold AI has to cross inside real organizations. A fraud alert, churn warning, inventory forecast, sales prediction, or clinical risk score does not become useful simply because it appears on a dashboard. It becomes useful when a manager can ask: What drove this? Can we trust it? What would change the outcome? Is the system noticing something meaningful, or dressing up noise in confident language? This is where AI moves from clever software to operating infrastructure.

- The Practical Lesson

The Vanderbilt paper is narrow. It comes from one medical center, one dataset, and one clinical problem. It does not prove that LLMs are ready to run high-stakes decisions on their own. But it does point toward a larger managerial standard. The future of AI in business will not be judged only by whether systems can answer questions or make predictions. It will be judged by whether their work is legible enough to govern. Not just intelligent output. Inspectable judgment.
