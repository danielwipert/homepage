New Paper: AI research agents are not failing at expert-level science. They are struggling with the everyday judgment that makes research trustworthy.

The AARRI-Bench team tested frontier models and agentic systems on 82 manually crafted research tasks designed to resemble the work expected of a capable research intern.

The strongest configuration, Mini-SWE-Agent with Claude Opus 4.7, reached a 68.3% success rate. The best-performing system still failed roughly one out of every three tasks.

What makes the benchmark interesting is not simply the score, but the kind of competence it is trying to measure. This is not another test of raw coding ability, retrieval, or generic task completion. AARRI-Bench is aimed at the quieter virtues of research work: context awareness, methodological care, academic integrity, and the ability to notice when a small detail changes the meaning of the entire task. These are precisely the areas where today’s agents remain brittle.

The implication is uncomfortable. Research agents are becoming better at producing research-shaped output, but they are not yet reliable research colleagues. Tool use can extend their reach, and scaffolding can improve their workflow, but neither automatically supplies judgment, field sensitivity, or the instinct to pause when something feels methodologically wrong.

For teams deploying AI research assistants, the lesson is practical rather than philosophical: keep humans close to the loop, especially when the work depends on nuance. In research, small errors rarely stay small. They compound quietly, and by the time they appear in the final analysis, they often look like confidence.

Paper: “Act As a Real Researcher: A Suite of Benchmarks Evaluating Frontier LLMs and Agentic Harnesses in Research Lifecycle,” Wang et al.
