Researchers tested 56 large language models and found that 81–90% of the differences in their apparent personalities may be nothing more than a measurement artifact.

For the past two years, researchers have assigned AI systems traits like risk aversion, agreeableness, political orientation, and personality using questionnaires originally designed for humans.

This paper argues that many of those conclusions may be built on a flawed premise.

Researchers from the Max Planck Institute for Human Development, the University of Konstanz, the Barcelona Supercomputing Center, and the University of Basel administered personality and risk-preference instruments to 56 instruction-tuned LLMs alongside large human reference samples.

They found that between-model differences were overwhelmingly explained by a simple directional response bias, a tendency to prefer one end of a scale or one labeled option regardless of the question itself.

The numbers are striking:

- 81–90% of between-model variation was explained by response bias
- Humans showed only 9–16% variation from the same effect
- Larger and more capable models reduced the bias, but never eliminated it

Many AI evaluations assume that if a model consistently scores as “risk seeking” or “highly agreeable,” it possesses a stable behavioral trait. This work suggests those profiles can change simply by changing which questions are asked or how they are phrased.

In other words, the benchmark may be measuring the benchmark as much as the model. That is a useful reminder for enterprise AI adoption.

As AI moves deeper into hiring, healthcare, finance, customer service, and research, evaluation becomes infrastructure. If the measurement system is biased, governance built on top of those measurements inherits the same weakness.

Paper: Apparent Psychological Profiles of Large Language Models are Largely a Measurement Artifact

Authors: Jelena Meyer, David Garcia, Dirk U. Wulff
