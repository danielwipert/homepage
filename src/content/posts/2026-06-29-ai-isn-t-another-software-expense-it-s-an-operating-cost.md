AI isn't another software expense; it's an operating cost. Here is why...

- Software is purchased. AI is operated.

Traditional software budgets begin with licenses, seats, and renewals. Once purchased, the economics are relatively stable until usage or headcount changes. AI starts with access, but it rarely ends there.

- The real cost begins after deployment.

Putting AI into production creates work that didn't exist before: integration, workflow redesign, human review, monitoring, governance, maintenance, and continuous improvement. Subscriptions are just an entrance fee.

- AI costs follow the actual work.

As more employees, customers, documents, transactions, or agents use AI, the operating cost changes with them. That's a fundamentally different budgeting problem than buying another software license. Tokens grow with work. More work, more tokens, more cost.

- Vendor pricing isn't deployment economics.

The cheapest model isn't always the cheapest implementation. One model may require more engineering. Another may need more human review. A third may introduce additional governance or operational overhead. The price on the vendor's website tells you remarkably little about the total cost of running AI inside your business.

- AI should be budgeted like an Operating Capability.

The question isn't simply: "What does the model cost?", "What does this workflow cost to operate?" That's where AI economics becomes more interesting than AI pricing.

The companies that gain the most from AI won't necessarily be the ones that buy the cheapest models. They'll be the ones that best understand the economics of operating them.
