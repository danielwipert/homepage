"To the young leaders surveyed, moral leaders show a strong commitment to their direct reports, individually and collectively. This was the single-most cited attribute."

I am pretty evangelical about this. The core responsibility of a manager is the well-being of the team, but I rarely have seen this as the top priority of the managers I have come across in my career.

People want to be leaders for all types of reasons. They want more money. They want to advance their career. They want to feel better about themselves, correcting some wrong or slight in their life. Nothing is wrong with that perse. But none of those drivers have anything to do with leadership. Those are possible feelings and rewards you get once you are a leader, but they certainly don't make great leaders.

One of the odd paradoxes I have noticed in my career is that the best leaders and managers are almost always people who have avoided leadership and people management roles. We have coded leadership and management in such a way that the core principles inherent to good leadership (empathy, trust, loyalty, collectiveness) have been excised in favor of the possible symptoms of leadership (power, wealth, prestige). We have confused the act of leading with the role of leading.
