The latest reading from the University of Michigan’s consumer sentiment survey has slipped to levels rarely seen outside periods of acute economic distress.

That alone would be notable. What makes it more interesting is the absence of a defining crisis. There is no financial system unraveling, no sudden economic shock. And yet, sentiment sits near historical lows.

Over decades, this survey has traced the emotional rhythm of the U.S. economy with remarkable consistency: confidence falls sharply in downturns, then recovers as conditions stabilize. Recently, that recovery has looked less convincing. The peaks are lower, the rebounds shorter, and the floor easier to reach.

It raises a quieter, more uncomfortable possibility. The issue may not be cyclical at all, but structural, a slow weakening in how households perceive their economic footing.
