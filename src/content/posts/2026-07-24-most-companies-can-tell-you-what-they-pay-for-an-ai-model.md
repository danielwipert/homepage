Most companies can tell you what they pay for an AI model. Far fewer can tell you what it costs to produce a usable business outcome...

The model bill is often only one visible line item inside a much larger operating process. Consider a customer-support workflow. An AI response might cost $0.18 to generate. But if it also requires $1.20 of human review, $0.55 of rework and $0.40 of escalation, the relevant economic number is not the cost of the response. It is the $2.33 cost of resolving the ticket.

The same logic applies elsewhere. Invoice automation should be measured by the cost of posting an invoice correctly, not the cost of extracting a field. A fraud system should be measured by the cost of reaching an accurate case disposition, not the cost of producing a score. An inventory tool should be measured by the cost and quality of an accepted replenishment decision, not the cost of generating a recommendation.

Once the denominator changes, the model decision can change with it. A more expensive model may be economically superior if it reduces reviews, exceptions, and rework. A cheaper model may simply transfer cost into the rest of the workflow.

The model bill still matters, especially at scale. But it is not the complete cost of AI. AI economics begins at the business outcome, not the model invoice.
