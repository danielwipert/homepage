There is a certain inevitability to the argument put forward in this paper, Small Language Models are the Future of Agentic AI. Simple is better.

The paper makes a radical claim, one that cuts against the grain of the current moment: most of what we ask AI systems to do is not grand, open-ended reasoning, but narrow, repetitive work. And for that, the authors argue, we are dramatically overpaying (in compute, in latency, in complexity) by routing everything through large, generalist models. I agree, and I have been arguing this side for at least a year now.

Small language models, they suggest, are already good enough. In many cases, they are not just sufficient, but preferable: faster, cheaper, and more adaptable, particularly when embedded inside agentic systems that break problems into smaller parts.

This is EXACTLY what I am building with Chorus AI Systems. Closed-loop, multi-agent systems. That is agentic AI.

The implication is not that large models disappear. It is that they recede. A shift from monoliths to systems: from a single, all-purpose intelligence to a collection of smaller, specialized ones, each doing its job with precision.

What is striking is how economic the argument is. A 7B model can be an order of magnitude cheaper to run than its larger counterparts, while still matching them on many of the tasks that actually matter in practice.

And so the question becomes less philosophical than operational: if most agentic work is narrow, why are we still architecting everything as if it were broad?

The future, if the authors are right, will not be defined by bigger models, but by better allocation, systems that know when to be large, and when to be small. Not more eloquence, but more discipline.
