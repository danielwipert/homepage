The May 2026 Customer Sentiment reading from the U of Michigan is very bad. The music has stopped, there are not enough chairs to go around...

Consumer sentiment fell to 44.8 in May, down 10% from April and 14.2% from a year ago. That puts it just below the prior historical trough from June 2022, when inflation had already done its work on the American psyche.

There is a temptation, especially amongst economists in comfortable shoes, to call this a “sentiment problem.” That is much too polite. What the survey is really capturing is the widening gap between egghead macroeconomic metrics and the lived experience of Americans. The gap is becoming a chasm.

Current economic conditions fell 12.8% month over month and 22.2% year over year. In plain English, people are not simply worried about tomorrow. They are having a bad day today.

Expectations fell too, down 8.3% from April. That matters because expectations are where the consumer economy starts to turn from optimism into self-protection. Households delay purchases. They trade down. They stop pretending the monthly budget is a puzzle they can solve with one more subscription cancellation. There is no point in rearranging the deck chairs on the Titantic.

The inflation numbers are the darker part of the report. Year-ahead inflation expectations rose to 4.8%, up from 4.7% in April and well above the 3.4% reading in February. Long-run inflation expectations climbed to 3.9%, compared with a 2.8% to 3.2% range throughout 2024.

That is not just a gasoline shock. That is inflation anxiety spreading into the bones of the household economy.

The most revealing figure is this: 57% of consumers spontaneously mentioned that high prices are eroding their personal finances, up from 50% last month. Spontaneously is doing a lot of work there. Nobody had to lead the witness. People volunteered the answer.

This is what happens when the cost of living becomes less a statistic than a daily negotiation with defeat. Food, fuel, rent, insurance, credit cards, repairs, childcare, healthcare. The great American household is still consuming, yes, but increasingly like a worker reporting for duty rather than a citizen enjoying prosperity.

The headline economy may still have its admirers. The labor market may still look respectable. GDP may still nod along politely. But the consumer, who is asked to carry the whole arrangement on their back, appears to be losing patience.

This is not yet a recession call. It is something subtler and perhaps more politically dangerous: a confidence recession inside an economy that still looks fine from 30,000 feet. And that, historically, is when the official data starts to sound less like reassurance and more like public relations.
