So this paper (The AI Layoff Trap) is making the rounds. I get the fireworks, since its a hot topic, but I have some counter thoughts...

Here is the basic idea:

When competing firms all automate using AI to cut labor costs, they collectively destroy the consumer demand that their own businesses depend on. Each firm individually makes a rational decision, but the collective result is irrational and harmful, to workers and to the firms themselves. It's a classic Prisoner's Dilemma.

The math behind it: workers spend more of their income than owners do (economists call this higher "marginal propensity to consume"). So when you replace workers with AI, spending in the economy falls. Each firm only absorbs a tiny fraction of that damage, but captures 100% of the cost saving, so every firm has a strong incentive to over-automate, even knowing the collective harm.

Their proposed fix: a Pigouvian automation tax: a tax calibrated to make each firm "feel" the full demand damage it's causing to the broader economy. They argue that UBI, profit-sharing, retraining programs, and capital taxes all fail to fix the core incentive problem.

A few of things to consider with this paper:

- It seems to conflate the cost of work vs cost of AI. It is not really that simple at all. Gen AI is still very messy. It could take 1000 agents to truly replace one FTE. I don't think the math here is simple and well-known right now.

- The paper does not have much analysis of what happens when the AI performs poorly, causes failures, or requires correction.

- Related to the above, the paper presupposes a clear and clean transition to Gen AI doing knowledge work. I think that is pollyannaish at best. Look at Amazon. They leaped into AI coders and lost 6 million orders in a single day. This is not going to be a smooth transition or quick.

- AI will create new industries and new jobs, not just take away jobs. They admit this in the paper, but it feels underplayed. Someone in my family was a street lamp lighter, you know when they were gas, who lost their job when electricity became widely available. He then worked for the railroad. Tech progress will always happen, human society evolves with it.

I think the biggest problem here is that the paper pinpoints a problem with laissez-faire capitalism and then suggests the solution is another market mechanism. I disagree. The solution will come from outside the market via collective organization, which will force representative government to enact stricter economic and social laws to protect workers. A new New Deal.
