City Economic Health Report: Inland Empire, CA

The Inland Empire, the vast Riverside–San Bernardino–Ontario metro east of Los Angeles, receives a C- in my latest economic assessment, placing it in the 31st percentile among the 50 largest US metropolitan areas. The grade, however, masks an unusually uneven economic profile.

Wage growth is exceptionally strong. Average earnings rose 8.7 per cent year on year, more than double the national metro rate of 4.2 per cent, a performance that would ordinarily indicate a buoyant labour market. Yet the region exhibits anything but strength across broader employment measures. Unemployment stands at 6.1 per cent, nearly two percentage points above the national average, while labour force participation of 60.2 per cent lags the benchmark by more than four points. Those who are employed are earning more; proportionally fewer residents are employed at all and more and more people are totally dropping out of the labor market.

This divergence reflects the Inland Empire’s evolution into Southern California’s logistics backbone. Amazon, warehouse operators and freight companies have competed aggressively for labour, bidding up wages even as job creation outside the sector remains subdued. The result is a bifurcated economy: robust for workers within the distribution ecosystem, markedly weaker for those outside it.

Housing indicators point to a market in retreat. The average home now sits for 63 days, nine days longer than the national figure, while prices per square foot have fallen 1.3 per cent, compared with a 0.3 per cent national decline. Building permits dropped 18.5 per cent year on year, signalling developer caution rather than a structural shortfall in supply.

Long marketed as an affordable alternative to coastal California, the Inland Empire now confronts a more complex value proposition: rising incomes for some residents, a cost-of-living score of 5.4 that exceeds the national average, and an employment landscape that offers fewer opportunities than most large metros. The economic foundation of the Inland Empire Metro seems on very shaky ground.
