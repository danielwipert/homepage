Much of the conversation around artificial intelligence still gravitates toward automation and which roles it will displace and which costs it will shave. That is an understandable instinct. It is also, I suspect, much too narrow.

The more interesting shift may lie in what Sangeet Paul Choudary calls the collapse of “translation costs”: the quiet friction involved in reconciling outputs across teams, tools, and data systems. These costs seldom appear in formal reporting, yet they determine how quickly and how coherently organizations can act.

AI begins to relax that constraint. By extracting structure from unstructured material (emails, PDFs, spreadsheets, images), it allows teams to maintain their own preferred workflows while intelligence systems translate between them. Coordination no longer requires labored consensus.

For incumbents whose advantage has rested on enforcing standards, that is unsettling. Control of the workflow becomes less decisive than control of the translation layer, or the ability to redefine accountability within a more fluid ecosystem.

If the argument holds, AI’s lasting contribution will not be automation inside silos, but the reorganization of how fragmented systems and teams converse with each other.
