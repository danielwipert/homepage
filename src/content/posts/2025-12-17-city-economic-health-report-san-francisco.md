📊 City Economic Health Report: San Francisco

San Francisco earns a "C" grade in my latest metro economic analysis, placing it in the 27th percentile among America's 50 largest metropolitan areas. For the city that bankrolled the digital age and commanded the highest valuations in American real estate, the grade represents something between a correction and a reckoning.

The headline is housing. Price per square foot fell 4.6% year-over-year, the steepest decline of any major American metro and more than fifteen times the national average drop of 0.3%. Building permits collapsed 40%, second only to Sacramento among large California metros. These are not minor fluctuations. San Francisco is experiencing a genuine repricing of its real estate market, one that reflects both elevated interest rates and a more fundamental reassessment of the city's post-pandemic value proposition.

Yet the labor market tells a more resilient story than the doom-loop narratives suggest. Unemployment of 4.8% sits just four-tenths above the national metro average: elevated, but hardly catastrophic. Labor force participation of 64.9% matches the national benchmark almost precisely. Wage growth of 4.3% actually exceeds the 3.5% metro average, suggesting that those who remain employed in the Bay Area continue to command premium compensation. The tech layoffs that dominated headlines have not, it appears, translated into broad labor market distress... yet. I imagine AI growth is propping this up.

The housing picture contains its own contradictions. Despite falling prices, homes sell in 50 days, a week faster than the national average. Buyers exist, and they are paying significantly less than a year ago. The pricing per sq foot has dropped significantly compared to the metro avg. Falling prices could restore the city's competitiveness, or they could signal that buyers see less value in San Francisco than they once did. Much depends on whether the city can address the downtown vacancy crisis and quality-of-life concerns that prompted the remote-work exodus.

San Francisco's cost of living score of 6.2 against a 4.8 national average remains punishing. But for the first time in a generation, the gap is narrowing from above rather than widening. The question facing the Bay Area is whether falling prices will restore competitiveness or do they reflect a diminishing demand for what the city offers.
