Capacity settles how much, and Allocation settles who. Treating the first as if it resolves the second is a mistake.

When an AI system cannot serve every request arriving at the same moment, the shortfall has to be divided. Some requests are processed immediately. Others enter a queue, are throttled, or fail. Some are moved ahead because they matter more.

Every one of those outcomes is an allocation. And allocation does not wait for someone to write a policy. If no rule has been set, arrival order becomes the rule and failure becomes the fallback. “We’ll deal with capacity if it becomes a problem” is therefore not a decision postponed. It is a decision to let the system’s default behavior determine who waits when demand is highest.

Capacity planning must answer two questions: How much simultaneous demand can the system serve? And when that limit is crossed, which work waits, slows down, or fails?

That second question is where business priorities become operating rules. A customer-facing decision, a compliance review, and an internal summary should not necessarily occupy the same place in the queue.

This does not mean capacity is always scarce, or that any one allocation rule is automatically fair or efficient. It means that when demand outruns capacity, allocation is unavoidable.

The design is optional. The allocation is not.
