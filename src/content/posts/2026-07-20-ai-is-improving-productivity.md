AI is improving productivity. But is that a clear ROI for a rapidly increasing AI spend…

Much of the current conversation about enterprise AI treats productivity as if it were the final measure of success. A team completes work faster, produces more output, or spends less time on routine tasks, and the conclusion seems obvious: the investment has paid off.

The actual real world economics of AI consumption are more complicated and nebulous than this common sense take.

Productivity is only the first step in a much longer chain. Time saved must become something the business can actually capture.

Productivity might increase throughput, reduce operating costs, improve quality, expand capacity, lower risk, or generate additional revenue. If none of those outcomes occur, the organization may simply have faster employees performing the same work. No value add has been actually captured.

This distinction matters because businesses do not invest in productivity for its own sake. They invest to create economic gain: value add is more than cost of AI.

That is why productivity should be treated as evidence of potential, not proof of ROI. The return exists only when the value created is captured, measured, and shown to exceed the full cost of adoption.

As AI becomes a larger part of everyday operations, I suspect this distinction will become increasingly important. The organizations that create lasting advantage will not be those that report the biggest productivity gains. They will be the ones that can explain, with discipline, how those gains became measurable business value.

Productivity is where the story begins. ROI is where it has to end.
