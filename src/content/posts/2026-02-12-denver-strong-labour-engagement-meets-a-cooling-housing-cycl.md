Denver: Strong labour engagement meets a cooling housing cycle

The Denver–Aurora–Centennial metropolitan area earns a solid B grade, underpinned by exceptional labour force engagement but tempered by a softening housing market.

The unemployment rate stands at 3.6%, modestly below the current US metro average of 3.9%, leaving Denver broadly in line with national conditions. On its own, this points to stability rather than outright strength.

The more striking figure is labour force participation. At 70.4%, versus a US metro average of 64.4%, Denver ranks in the upper tier nationally. This unusually high level of workforce engagement is the city’s clearest structural advantage. Participation at this level typically signals demographic vitality, in-migration of working-age residents, and a labour market capable of absorbing talent.

Wage growth reinforces that picture. Earnings are rising at +6.6% year-on-year, well above the US metro average of +3.5%. Combined with elevated participation, this suggests a labour market that is not merely tight, but competitive.

However, the housing data provides an important counterweight.  
Building permits are up +15% year-on-year, more than double the US metro average of +6.8%, suggesting continued developer confidence and forward-looking supply expansion. Yet prices are moving in the opposite direction. Housing cost per square foot has declined –3.4% year-on-year, compared with a marginal –0.18% nationally. Homes are also taking longer to sell, with average days on market at 72 days, slightly above the US metro average of 69 days.

This combination—rising permits, falling price per square foot, and a modestly extended selling period—signals a market in adjustment. Supply appears to be outpacing demand at current price levels, easing upward pressure and slowing transaction velocity.

The labour picture, too, carries a cautionary signal. Weekly hours worked average 33.4 hours, below the US metro average of 34.0 hours. In labour-market cycles, employers often trim hours before reducing headcount, making the workweek an early indicator of softening demand even when unemployment remains low. It can also point to underemployment (workers in jobs, but not getting the hours they want) which is consistent with evidence that involuntary part-time work can rise as cyclical weakness emerges.

The broader picture is coherent. Denver’s economy is anchored by unusually strong labour-force participation and above-average wage growth. Yet the housing sector is clearly in a cooling phase, and the shorter workweek suggests the labour market may be less uniformly robust than the headline figures imply.

In short, Denver remains fundamentally healthy, but the centre of gravity has shifted from housing-driven momentum to labour-market resilience, with early signs that demand may be normalising at the margin.
