Just finished Chapter 2 of my digital textbook, AI Operations Management. This chapter is entitled The Flow. We will see if I stick with that metaphor long-term, but I think it captures what I am trying to say right now.

The Flow is essentially how business actually works. It is a T-shirt getting made in China, then moving on a boat to Los Angeles and then to your warehouse in Louisville. It is the invoice getting sent via email, then passed around your team, then the money getting wired via ACH to a holding company. And on and on.

One of the more startling aspects of Generative AI is how it emerged from basic mathematics and computer science principles, yet it seems to not play by any of the rules of its creator. That is, AI as we know it is not traditional computing. It is a new form of computing, language processing, and communication. That sounds heady and a bit much, but I genuinely believe that to be true. Gen AI is revolutionary, but not because it can conjure a video of a grizzly bear shredding on a guitar.

Rather, Gen AI allows us to actually process data and language in a way that feels more natural. In terms of my textbook, I am trying to incorporate and predict how this amazing new tool will be used in business operations. It is not easy, nor would I say I have a crystal ball. But so much work has already been done with Applied AI that there are very clear and conservative rules and lessons we can draw from the current use cases.

The core of Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 of the book are simply this: stop thinking that Gen AI is the same thing as software. It is not. It works entirely different than software applications. Chapter 1 was about accepting this difference. Chapter 2 is starting to map out what makes AI different and how we might approach the management of AI in real world business. My expertise is not technical, but I am an expert in building and running businesses. The book is my attempt to build a bridge between the technical world of AI and the everyday reality of business operations.
