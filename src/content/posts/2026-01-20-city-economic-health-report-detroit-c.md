City Economic Health Report: Detroit -- C+

Economic indicators for the Detroit–Warren–Dearborn metropolitan area point to a very uneven recovery. The unemployment rate, at 5.1 per cent, places the region in the 8th percentile among peer metros, signalling relatively elevated joblessness. Labour force participation stands at 61.07 per cent, in the 22nd percentile, reinforcing the picture of a labour market that remains quite underpowered. Year-on-year earnings growth of 2.74 per cent, also in the 22nd percentile suggest severe sluggishness in the job market. Detroit also has a bizarrely high cost of living compared to other metros of its size. Metroes like Milwaukee and St Louis have a much cheaper cost of living. All in all, the Detroit job market looks rather bleak: high employment, low participation (very telling), low wage growth, high cost of living for size.

Housing data offer a partial counterweight. Building permits rose 38.25 per cent year on year, placing Detroit in the 88th percentile and pointing to renewed momentum in new construction. Home prices increased by a modest 3.5 per cent, ranking in the 38th percentile and suggesting restrained appreciation rather than overheating. At the same time, homes are spending an average of 54 days on the market—an 84th-percentile outcome—indicating slower transaction velocity, potentially reflecting cautious demand or frictions in supply.

The composite picture is one of limited progress. An overall weighted percentile score of 39.5, equivalent to a C+ grade, underscores the gap between pockets of strength in residential construction and a broader economy still constrained by weak labour fundamentals. While the data capture current conditions, they stop short of clarifying whether recent housing activity marks the beginning of a durable upswing or merely a cyclical pause within a longer period of adjustment.
