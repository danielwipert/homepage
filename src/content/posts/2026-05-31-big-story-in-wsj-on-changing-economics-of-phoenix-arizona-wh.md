Big story in WSJ on changing economics of Phoenix, Arizona — what else could be going on?

The WSJ piece argues that Phoenix is a warning sign for the AI economy: the city built a huge middle-class base on back-office cubicle jobs (call centers, claims processing, payroll, data entry, customer support) and those are exactly the repetitive white-collar tasks AI is starting to automate.

Phoenix may still grow through chips, data centers, and advanced manufacturing, but those jobs require different skills and may not absorb the workers displaced from administrative work. The WSJ punchline: AI may not just kill jobs; it may break one of the main ladders into the middle class. Really, WSJ?

Here is what I am seeing in Phoenix via my City Economic Health Report, a lot of red flags:

- Unemployment is higher that most USA major metros sitting at 4%
- Huge red flag is the contraction of Labor Force, dropping 1% year over year according to the latest numbers. That is not great. This means not only less employed people, but fewer people even looking for work now. This is also much worse than the drop in employees. That is a systemic shift that has more to do with macroeconomics and demographic changes.
- Another big red flag to me is that Weekly Hours worked is dropping. So we have the dreaded three drops: employees, labor force, and hours worked. That signals a very soft labor market in Phoenix, matching what the WSJ is saying, but the source to me is much bigger and deeper than AI.
- Wage Growth is ok, sitting at 3.7% year over year, around 50% percentile
- Building Permits are down 10% year over year, and houses are sitting on the market about 10% longer than this time last year

The picture is pretty clear. Phoenix is going through a contraction period after massive growth during the last 40 years. It popped off during Covid, and maybe now it is coming back down to earth. Blaming that on AI is a very odd take, though. This seems more like the natural cycles of boom cities.
