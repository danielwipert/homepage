New AI Paper: AI could be a rising tide lifting all boats, not a rogue wave destroying all jobs

Discussions of artificial intelligence often frame progress as a series of sudden breakthroughs, moments where systems cross a threshold and entire categories of work fall away all at once. This paper makes a quieter, and more consequential, claim.

AI does not advance in jagged discontinuities. It improves across thousands of real-world tasks at roughly the same rate, regardless of their duration or complexity. Short tasks improve, but longer ones move with them. The curve shifts upward as a whole. The system does not leap, it slowly rises.

“The main insight of this paper is that… the relationship between AI success on tasks and task duration is… more consistent with a rising tide rather than a crashing wave.”

That distinction matters greatly. A crashing wave draws attention because it is visible and localized. A rising tide works differently. It spreads across everything at once. It does not eliminate one type of work at a time. It applies steady pressure across many forms of work simultaneously.

“Models perform, on average, not dramatically differently on short versus long tasks.”

The pace of the rising tide remains fast. If current trends continue, most text-based tasks could reach sufficient automation, something like 80 to 95 percent success, within the next several years. Not perfect, but good enough to change how work gets done.

“Most text-related tasks [could reach] success rates of 80%–95% by 2029.”

For those building systems, the constraint has already shifted. Models can handle more than we often assume. The real problem now lies in how we structure systems that can take imperfect components and make them reliable at scale.
